Failure cases

emir.tif initially failed to align using raw-pixel NCC/L2. The scene's blue and pink robes are very bright in some channels but dark in others, so the metric latched onto a displacement where the brightness patterns happened to line up by coincidence, rather than the true structural alignment — producing a heavily ghosted result. It was fixed by scoring alignment on Sobel edge maps instead of raw pixel values, along with a wider search window and refinement radius to give the pyramid more room to correct a bad coarse-level guess. See the Better Features write-up below for details.

04

Bells & Whistles

Required for CS280A. Each item below shows a before/after comparison and an explanation of the approach.

Automatic Cropping

Approach

Rather than cropping a fixed percentage off every side, I scan inward from each edge and measure the standard deviation of brightness across each row and column. A genuine content row has real variation; a border row (black, white, or a flat colored strip) is nearly uniform. I trim rows/columns until I hit real content, capping how far in the scan is allowed to go so a uniform area in the middle of a real photo (like a plain sky) never gets eaten away.

Automatic Contrasting

Approach

For each channel, I rescale so the 1st percentile brightness maps to 0 and the 99th percentile maps to 1, rather than using the literal min/max. Using percentiles instead of true extremes makes the stretch robust to a handful of stray dust specks or scratches on the glass plate, which would otherwise compress the contrast of the rest of the image.

Automatic White Balance

Approach

I used the gray-world assumption: on average, a natural scene's colors should balance out to neutral gray. I compute the mean brightness of each of the R, G, B channels, then scale each channel so its mean matches the overall gray-level mean. This cancels out a consistent color cast without needing to identify a specific "white" object in the scene.

Better Color Mapping

Approach

Instead of assuming the B, G, R glass plate exposures already share the same brightness distribution before stacking them into a color image, I used histogram matching to remap the G and R channels' brightness distributions to match B's. This corrects for the fact that Prokudin-Gorskii's red and green filters didn't necessarily respond with the same overall tonal curve as the blue filter — there's no reason the three plates should already agree on brightness statistics. This isn't the only valid approach; I'd also be curious to try searching over per-channel gain weights directly.

Better Features (Edge/Gradient-Based Alignment)

Approach

For emir.tif, I replaced raw-pixel comparison with Sobel edge maps: I compute the gradient magnitude of each channel first, then run NCC/L2 on those edge maps instead of the original pixel values. Edge maps capture where the outlines and boundaries of objects are, which is consistent across channels even when their brightness patterns differ a lot — exactly emir.tif's problem, where the same robe is bright in one channel and dark in another.

05

Reflection

The core alignment algorithm — exhaustive search plus an image pyramid — worked well on most of the collection right away, but emir.tif was a good reminder that "align by pixel similarity" quietly assumes the channels agree on brightness, which isn't always true. Debugging it took a few layers: first confirming the displacement search was even finding a reasonable answer, then widening the pyramid's refinement radius so a bad coarse-level guess could still be corrected, and finally switching to edge-based scoring so brightness mismatches stopped misleading the metric altogether. The Bells & Whistles ended up compounding nicely — better color mapping and white balance made the final images look far more like real photographs than the raw channel stack ever could, and automatic cropping cleaned up the fringing at the edges where the three channels don't perfectly overlap after alignment.
